Re-Roofing Metairie's Post-War Ranches

Most of Metairie went up after the war, which means block after block of low, wide single-story homes with shallow pitches and deep eaves. Shallow pitch is unforgiving: water moves off it slowly, so a lifted shingle or a tired valley has time to find a way in rather than being flushed away.

That changes where we look first. On a Metairie roof the valleys and the soffit are the usual entry points, not the open field of the roof where people tend to point. It also changes what matters in the build. On a shallow pitch the membrane underneath is what actually keeps a ceiling dry once wind lifts the top layer, and that is the line most cheap quotes leave thin.

The Lake, the Wind, and Keeping Water Out

Metairie sits low and flat between the river and Lake Pontchartrain. Wind comes off the lake with very little to break it, and the humidity that follows a storm never really leaves. Deep eaves with blocked soffit vents then cook the deck from below, and a cooked deck shortens a new roof by years.

So ventilation gets checked on every Metairie inspection, not only when someone asks. It is the cheapest thing on the estimate and the one most likely to decide whether the roof reaches its rated life.
