What Metairie Roofs
Are Up Against

Metairie sits between the lake and the river on the Jefferson Parish side, which means two things for a roof. Wind comes off Lake Pontchartrain with very little to break it, and the humidity that follows a storm never really leaves.

Most of the housing stock here is post-war single storey with shallow pitches and wide eaves. Shallow pitch is unforgiving: water moves off it slowly, so a lifted shingle or a tired valley has time to find a way in. Wide eaves mean soffit and fascia take a beating that nobody notices until the paint starts to blister.

What That Changes About the Work

We check the valleys and the soffit first. On a Metairie roof those two are the usual entry points, not the field of the roof where people look.

Underlayment matters more than the shingle. On a shallow pitch the membrane underneath is what actually keeps the ceiling dry once wind lifts the top layer. It is also the line most cheap quotes leave thin.

Ventilation gets checked every time. Wide eaves with blocked soffit vents cook the deck from below, and a cooked deck shortens a new roof by years.
